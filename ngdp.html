Reading the Horizons

Recent decisions

Shorter windows emphasize more recent expectations underlying production and employment plans.

Projects underway

Intermediate windows retain expectations that may remain relevant as investments progress toward completion or abandonment.

Longer commitments

Longer windows retain older expectations that may be embedded in financing obligations and other commitments.

These are interpretations to investigate. The data measure spending relative to earlier forecast paths; they do not directly observe the duration of projects or contracts, or identify the causal effect of monetary policy.

Methodology & sources

How the measure is constructed

One method, seventeen windows

For a window of W quarters, the benchmark averages the available survey forecasts for the target quarter, using survey origins from the current quarter through t − (W − 1), with equal weight per survey. SPF contributes W quarterly surveys. Livingston contributes only its actual June and December surveys: five surveys in a ten-quarter window. An eleven-quarter window contains five or six Livingston surveys, depending on the target quarter. Increasing the window by one quarter can leave Livingston’s forecast set unchanged when the added quarter has no survey. Every setting uses the same GDP vintage and forecast-extension rules.

NGDP gap (%) = 100 × (actual nominal GDP ÷ average forecast − 1)

A positive gap means spending exceeds the benchmark; a negative gap means it falls short. The benchmark moves forward each quarter. A negative gap in the later 2010s does not preserve a fixed pre-crisis spending path.

Forecasts, extrapolation, and historical coverage

The line uses median Livingston Survey forecasts for the early history and switches to the Philadelphia Fed’s Survey of Professional Forecasters (SPF) at its first complete window. SPF takes priority whenever a complete window is available. Reducing the window moves the SPF start earlier and replaces Livingston observations with SPF observations for those dates. Livingston ends in the preceding quarter, so the two series never overlap. Values are joined directly, without smoothing or an adjustment at the transition. Forecast growth paths are rebased onto a common revised GDP vintage.

Beyond the reported quarterly horizon, forecasts use long-run real GDP growth and CPI inflation, adjusted by the terminal short-run difference between GDP-deflator and CPI inflation. Where those long-run forecasts are unavailable, the last available quarterly growth rate is extended. Longer windows consequently use more extrapolated forecasts.

Livingston is surveyed twice a year. Missing quarterly targets are interpolated in logs within each survey’s forecast path; quarterly surveys are not fabricated. For the early Livingston extension, forecasts beyond the reported horizon extend the growth between the six- and twelve-month forecasts. The SPF reported GNP before 1992, and the early Livingston forecasts also refer to GNP; their forecast growth is used as a proxy for GDP growth in this reconstruction. Only complete windows are shown, so the starting date changes with the slider. Recession shading uses the NBER-based USREC indicator.

The early Livingston extension is provisional. The Philadelphia Fed cautions against computing forecast growth from historical base values before June 2004. Uncertainty in these bases, the GNP-to-GDP approximation, persistent forecast errors, and extrapolation can affect sustained patterns as well as individual quarters. The broad direction of the early gap therefore remains subject to validation. Agreement with SPF in overlapping years supports comparability but does not independently validate the 1950s and 1960s estimates. Positive gaps indicate spending above the reconstructed benchmark; attributing them to monetary accommodation requires additional historical evidence.

Sources and relationship to published measures

The approach draws on Beckworth (2020), Luther (2024), and Martinez et al. (2026). This explorer is based on Martinez, Schibuola, and Beckworth (2026), extended to compare alternative planning and commitment horizons using forecast windows of four to twenty quarters. It is not an exact numerical replication of the published series; data vintages and implementation can affect the values.

Methodological and historical limitations

The Livingston extension is a reconstruction from semiannual surveys. A ten-quarter window contains five Livingston surveys, compared with ten quarterly SPF surveys. Missing quarterly forecast targets are estimated within each survey’s forecast path by assuming steady compounded growth between reported points. Forecasts beyond the reported horizon require extrapolation; the early Livingston extension carries forward growth between the six- and twelve-month forecasts.

The Philadelphia Fed’s Livingston documentation cautions against computing forecast growth from historical base values before June 2004. Early forecasts also refer to nominal GNP, whose forecast growth is used here as a proxy for GDP growth. Uncertainty in historical bases, that approximation, persistent forecast errors, and extrapolation can affect sustained patterns as well as individual quarters. Agreement with SPF in overlapping years supports comparability but does not independently validate the 1950s and 1960s estimates.

A positive gap indicates spending above the reconstructed forecast benchmark; a negative gap indicates spending below it. The gap alone does not establish the timing or causal effect of monetary easing or tightening. The broad direction of the early historical estimates remains subject to validation, and interpreting them as evidence of monetary accommodation requires additional historical evidence. Classification bands are descriptive thresholds, not confidence intervals.
