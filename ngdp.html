Reading the Horizons

Recent decisions

Shorter windows emphasize more recent expectations underlying production and employment plans.

Projects underway

Intermediate windows retain expectations that may remain relevant as investments progress toward completion or abandonment.

Longer commitments

Longer windows retain older expectations that may be embedded in financing obligations and other commitments.

These are interpretations to investigate. The data measure spending relative to earlier forecast paths; they do not directly observe the duration of projects or contracts, or identify the causal effect of monetary policy.

Methodology & sources

How the measure is constructed

One method, seventeen windows

For a window of W quarters, the benchmark averages W survey vintages, from the current quarter through t − (W − 1), with equal weights. Every setting uses the same sources, GDP vintage, and forecast-extension rules. Only the window length changes.

NGDP gap (%) = 100 × (actual nominal GDP ÷ average forecast − 1)

A positive gap means spending exceeds the benchmark; a negative gap means it falls short. The benchmark moves forward each quarter. A negative gap in the later 2010s does not preserve a fixed pre-crisis spending path.

Forecasts, extrapolation, and historical coverage

The underlying data are median forecasts from the Philadelphia Fed’s Survey of Professional Forecasters (SPF). Forecast growth paths are rebased onto a common revised GDP vintage. Missing internal forecast levels are interpolated in logs.

Beyond the reported quarterly horizon, forecasts use long-run real GDP growth and CPI inflation, adjusted by the terminal short-run difference between GDP-deflator and CPI inflation. Where those long-run forecasts are unavailable, the last available quarterly growth rate is extended. Longer windows consequently use more extrapolated forecasts.

The SPF reported GNP before 1992; its forecast growth is used as a proxy for GDP growth in this reconstruction. Only complete windows are shown, so the starting date changes with the slider. Recession shading uses the NBER-based USREC indicator.

Sources and relationship to published measures

The approach draws on Beckworth (2020), Luther (2024), and Martinez et al. (2026). This explorer is based on Martinez, Schibuola, and Beckworth (2026), extended to compare alternative planning and commitment horizons using forecast windows of four to twenty quarters. It is not an exact numerical replication of the published series; data vintages and implementation can affect the values.

The Luther link opens his technical description, which cites the 2024 working paper, “Neutral Nominal Spending and the Nominal Spending Gap.”
